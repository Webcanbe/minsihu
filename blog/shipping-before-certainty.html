Shipping Before Certainty

Earlier SaaS experiment · May 2026

In May 2026 I built a working SaaS prototype in roughly a day and a half. It analyzed startup ideas, surfaced risks, proposed execution plans, and tracked progress.

The build was fast enough that the main question immediately changed. Once the software existed, there was no value in continuing to polish it privately. The next uncertainty was distribution.

Speed is useful when it changes the question

Fast building is often treated as a virtue by itself. I think the better test is whether shipping removes an uncertainty.

Before the prototype existed, I did not know whether I could turn the concept into a coherent working flow quickly. After it existed, that question was answered. The remaining uncertainty was whether anyone in the target market would care enough to try it.

Distribution can be designed around constraints

I was targeting the U.S. market and considering short-form distribution, but I did not want the plan to depend on being good at traditional video production. That pushed me toward faceless formats: interface recordings, text-led hooks, short demonstrations, and clear before-and-after outcomes.

The constraint was useful because it forced the marketing format to stay close to the product. Instead of producing lifestyle content around the idea of entrepreneurship, the content could show the software doing something.

The lesson I kept

I did not treat the prototype as proof of a business. A functioning product proves that a product can function. It does not prove demand, retention, pricing, or distribution.

What I kept from that experiment is a simple rule: build fast enough to reach the next real uncertainty, then stop building until you have learned something about it.
