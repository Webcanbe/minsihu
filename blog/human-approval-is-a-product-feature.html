Human Approval Is a Product Feature

Returns & refunds workflow · May–June 2026

I explored a returns and refunds system for Shopify stores where the interesting problem was not generating a refund decision. The interesting problem was deciding when software should be allowed to act.

A refund has real cost, policy implications, customer impact, and edge cases. That makes it a useful test for human-in-the-loop product design.

Rules first, AI second

The system separated deterministic policy rules from softer judgment. Order age, product type, return window, and known exceptions can often be expressed directly. AI becomes more useful around ambiguous descriptions or prioritization, but it should not silently override hard constraints.

I began thinking in thresholds: below one level, the system can suggest; above another, it must ask for human approval. That is a more useful mental model than simply adding an AI button.

Automation should expose responsibility

The workflow should make it obvious who made the final decision and why. That means keeping the rules, model suggestion, confidence or rationale, and final human action separate enough to audit later.

The lesson carried into other projects: the more consequential the action, the more the product needs an explicit permission and accountability layer.
