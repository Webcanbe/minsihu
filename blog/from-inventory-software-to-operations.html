From Inventory Software to Operations

Earlier product experiment · April 2026

One of my earlier SaaS projects started as inventory software. The first version looked like the category usually looks: products, stock levels, suppliers, alerts, and sync.

The useful lesson came when I asked what the user was actually paying to avoid. The answer was not “looking at inventory.” It was the operational work created by inventory: noticing risk, deciding what to order, approving a purchase, and keeping store data synchronized.

From dashboard to workflow

I connected store data, worked through Shopify OAuth and API integration, and moved away from mock data toward real product and inventory states. Alerts were tied to concrete conditions such as stock falling below safety stock or an expected stockout arriving before lead time.

That made the dashboard more accurate, but accuracy alone still did not complete the job. The valuable moment was when the system could turn a risk into an action: create an order proposal, let the user approve it, and track the result.

Manual can be a feature in an MVP

I also learned to stop treating manual steps as automatically bad. For an early product, a manual sync or approval can be the fastest way to validate the workflow before investing in a fully automated background system.

The important question is whether the manual step blocks learning. If it does not, it can keep the architecture simpler while the product is still discovering what should be automated.

The category was not the insight

I never became attached to “inventory SaaS” as an identity. The interesting insight was broader: operational software becomes much more valuable when it closes the loop between information and action.

That idea still shows up in the way I think about products now. A dashboard tells you what happened. A workflow helps you decide what happens next.
